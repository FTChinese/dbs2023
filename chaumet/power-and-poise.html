Power
& poise

When it comes to self-expression, the Chaumet tiara always rises to the occasion

From 1805 onwards, Marie-Étienne Nitot, the founder of Chaumet, collaborated with Joséphine Bonaparte, the first wife of Napoleon I, to create some of the most audacious jewellery of the era – decadent parures that displayed the new empress’s impeccable taste and stature. The tiara was a part of each set – a precious power move that enhanced the simpler hairstyles of her day.

Centuries later, the sentiment and symbolism endure. The tiara remains a mainstay at Chaumet. It is present in each high-jewellery collection and is still the ultimate bespoke commission. Sitting front and centre of the silhouette, it embodies multitudes: a projection of empowerment, along with a love for fashion and romance. Its wearers are wide-reaching, from those comfortable in the spotlight – such as Amanda Seyfried, Song Hye-Kyo and the Olympic athlete and model Eileen Gu – to discreet powerbrokers in the worlds of business and politics. All see a tiara as a chance to rise to the occasion, to portray and feel the best version of themselves.

Given its significance as an emblem and a status symbol, in 2010 Chaumet’s Creative Studio sought to transform the tiara into something more accessible – to translate that same feeling and promise into a wider collection. “It was really very spontaneous: we had received a request to design a cocktail ring, and we thought about how tiaras are an emblem of the house and a status symbol,” says Olga Corsini, the director of the studio. “We thought, ‘Why not create a tiara in a ring?’”

Corsini is sitting in the creative studio in Paris’s 8th arrondissement. Her first tenure was at Chaumet from 2007 to 2011, and she returned in 2024. She says it was easy to pick up where she left off: “We have such an incredible story that 10 years is nothing compared to 250.”

From that first ring, with its pear-cut diamond and the V-shaped aigrette – now signatures of the collection – a full jewellery offering evolved, defined by an elegance and lightness that play out across an array of earrings, bracelets and graphic pendants in white gold with pearls and diamonds. The tiara has become a signature, an emblem whose elegant form can be worn in a multitude of places on the body.

Thus, heritage is married with contemporary ways of wearing. The collection embraces asymmetrical forms and styles designed to be combined through stacking. The line also introduced a pear- or tear-shaped watch with a pavé- or mirror-polished gold case and a slender strap that wraps around the wrist without a buckle.

Corsini says it is important to honour a certain spirit while shrugging off any formal protocol. “The reason we chose Joséphine as a name, and not a title like empress, is because it embodies the idea of a feminine French woman that almost everybody can relate to.”

Bridal, of course, is an important part of the picture. For Chaumet clients, the wedding day is a fairytale moment when the bride can truly shine, while acquiring a trousseau offers the new family the chance to start a legacy collection. (In 1986 Diana Ross bought her own Chaumet tiara to wear when she married her husband Arne Naess.)

In the Joséphine collection, the Valse Impériale parure is the apex of the suite and includes the tiaras. “Joséphine is always an expression of how a tiara can be worn in a modern way,” Corsini says. One style features perfect symmetry of three rows of diamonds in a fil-couteau mount, finished with a line of pear-shaped cuts that sweep outward to elevate the silhouette – a showcase of the artistry of the 12 Vendôme workshop.

All the high jewellery at Chaumet starts its life here, in the design studio, home to a small team of 12 creatives – including two members who were once hands-on in the workshop. “Our collaboration with the atelier is already so close, we spend half our time over there, but I am really interested in having this kind of profile in my team because they know what is technically possible. This is a true craft,” Corsini says, adding: “We are like architects – we oversee the design until its completion.”

In addition to the permanent collections such as Joséphine, each year the team conceives a special high jewellery collection (some 50 styles) based around a new theme. The genesis is a few evocative words from which will be conjured a visual mood board and early sketches that begin the long process of the creation, production and transmission of what will become modern masterpieces.

In the multi-generational mix of the studio some, like Corsini, sketch by hand; others rely on the iPad. Still, some analogue traditions remain effective: the near-final sketch of a tiara can be drawn to scale on paper, then simply cut out and placed on the head. “Most of the time when we do this, we get a real sense of how it will fit and look from all angles, and whether the volumes are correct. It’s simple but smart,” she says. Alternatively, the artisans hand sculpt traditional waxes, shaping and carving to create detailed, true-to-scale, three-dimensional prototypes and carefully arranging the as-yet unset diamonds and precious stones within the maquette to evaluate the design, volume and movement of the piece. A design can also be printed as a 3D model in resin or alloy, before making its way to the master jewellers in the workshop who refine these models by hand before metal casting.

For 2026, the idea of nature – a leitmotif for the house – took a new direction. “We thought about the world of spices, because it allows for such a new interpretation,” Corsini says, pulling out a gouache (a hand-painted watercolour) of a tiara they presented in April (and already sold), based around the theme of vanilla. It is a thin band crafted from white gold and adorned with petals pavé-set with brilliant-cut diamonds, and yellow diamond pistils to represent the flower.

“A bespoke order can culminate in something Chaumet calls the Tiara Experience”

Of course, there is the flower, the beans that inspired the form, but there is also the notion of vanilla – it is sweet, and that is something subjective and intangible, because it makes a connection with a memory, a sensation that is personal and not visible,” she says of the no-holds-barred creativity that drives a team of artisans who work from heart to hand.

Inspiration for bespoke commissions can also be drawn from the maison’s rich source: the thousands of creations recorded in its archives – some 66,000 sketches or hundreds of 3D alloy maquettes generated across its nearly 250-year history. There are established codes and recurring motifs from the world of nature that underpin the visual language – a richness that is constantly reinvented for the contemporary client. Throughout history, the house has reimagined the form with tiaras that can be transformed into a brooch (The Leuchtenberg tiara designed by Fossin, 1830-1840), a hair ornament (the Dahlia tiara from the Jewels by Nature collection, 2025) and, most recently, a mask (the Envol tiara of 2026) – each incarnation a demonstration of the workshop’s technical mastery.

The utmost care is put into projecting how the wearer might feel when they put on one of these unique pieces. A bespoke order can culminate in something Chaumet calls the Tiara Experience – an immersive ritual designed to help the transformation. Clients are fitted in their new tiara while wearing their chosen outfit, with complete hair and make-up styled to match. Then a ballet dancer from the Paris Opera is brought in to guide posture and movement – how to walk, dance and hold oneself in general. A little taller to be sure, and with a greater sense of confidence and strength.
